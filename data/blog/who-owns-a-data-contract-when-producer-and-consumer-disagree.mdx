“The producer owns the contract” sounds decisive. I used to like this answer because it gives one team responsibility. Then I worked on enough integrations to see the missing half: the producer knows what it emits, but the consumer knows what it depends on.

If one team owns everything, either consumers become invisible or producers lose the ability to evolve. I now treat contract ownership as a small agreement between different kinds of authority.

## Four things are hiding inside one contract

When teams say “schema”, they often mix these questions:

```text
What bytes or fields can be sent?
What does each value mean?
Which behaviours do consumers rely on?
How is a change proposed, observed, and retired?
```

These are related but they do not have the same owner.

My default split is:

| Concern | Primary owner | Required participants |
| --- | --- | --- |
| emitted representation | producer | platform/schema tooling |
| consumer assumptions | each consumer | producer |
| business definition | domain owner | producers and consumers |
| compatibility policy | shared governance | affected teams |
| migration deadline | accountable product/engineering lead | all live consumers |

“Shared governance” does not mean a meeting where nobody decides. It means the decision process is owned, while individual facts still have named owners.

## The producer cannot see every dependency

Imagine an event:

```json
{
  "account_id": "a-19",
  "status": "active",
  "updated_at": "2026-12-07T08:00:00Z"
}
```

The producer may define `status` as the current operational state. One consumer sends email when it becomes active. Another counts active accounts for billing. A third treats `updated_at` as a synchronization cursor, even though the producer never promised that it is unique or monotonic.

The third dependency may be a mistake, but it is still a production fact. Deleting it from a document does not delete it from the system.

This is why I ask consumers to publish their assumptions:

```text
fields read
enum values handled
ordering assumptions
absence/null behaviour
latency and freshness expectation
replay and duplicate behaviour
business decision made from the data
```

The producer can then challenge an unsupported assumption before it becomes permanent.

## Business meaning needs a domain owner

Neither transport team should privately decide what “active” means if invoices, access, and compliance depend on it.

I want a definition closer to:

```text
ACTIVE: the account may use paid product capabilities.
It does not prove that the latest invoice is settled.
It becomes effective at lifecycle_effective_at.
```

Now consumers can decide whether `ACTIVE` is sufficient for their use. The definition may still evolve, but the change is visible as a business change, not disguised as a harmless enum edit.

This is also why [semantic versioning alone does not describe data compatibility](/blog/why-semantic-versioning-does-not-describe-data-compatibility). A version number cannot tell me which decision a consumer makes.

## I use compatibility in both directions

A change can be safe for a new reader and unsafe for an old one. It can also be safe for live traffic but unsafe for historical replay.

For each proposal I record:

| Question | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Can old consumers read new data? | unknown enum value handled? |
| Can new consumers read old data? | new required field absent? |
| Can old stored records be replayed? | default still has same meaning? |
| Can a rollback emit the old shape? | producer deployment reversible? |
| Does meaning remain compatible? | same field drives same decision? |

The [Protocol Buffers guide](https://protobuf.dev/programming-guides/proto3/#updating) documents concrete wire-evolution rules, including field-number discipline. These rules are necessary when using Protobuf. They still do not answer the semantic questions in the last column.

## A change proposal should carry evidence

I prefer a short contract-change record over a long architecture ceremony:

```yaml
change: add lifecycle_effective_at
producer: accounts
business_owner: subscriptions
compatibility: optional for old readers
historical_default: updated_at
known_consumers:
  - access-control
  - billing-export
observation_window: 30 days
rollback: continue emitting old and new fields
retirement_condition: no reads of inferred timestamp
```

The record tells reviewers what evidence is missing. It also survives team changes better than a chat thread.

Consumer-driven contract tests can help expose expected interactions; the [Pact documentation](https://docs.pact.io/) describes this collaboration model. I still keep producer schema tests and domain-semantic tests. A mocked consumer expectation does not prove the producer's stored history can be migrated.

## Disagreement is useful information

If a producer says a change is compatible and a consumer says it is breaking, I do not resolve it by asking who is more senior. I ask what assumption differs.

Usually the disagreement belongs to one of these groups:

- The consumer relies on behaviour never documented.
- The producer changed meaning but only reviewed shape.
- The consumer cannot migrate within the proposed window.
- The producer cannot support two forms safely.
- Nobody owns the business definition.

Each group has a different fix. Undocumented behaviour may need temporary support plus a migration. A semantic change may need a new event or field. An impossible dual-write may require a coordinated cutover.

## Runtime observation completes the contract

A registry tells me which schemas are registered. It may not tell me which fields live consumers actually use.

Where privacy and cost allow, I add safe observations:

```text
consumer identity and version
schema version received
unknown value count
deprecated field use
decode or semantic rejection
last successful processing point
```

I do not log full payloads just to discover usage. Field-level counters and explicit consumer declarations are often enough.

## My answer to “who owns it?”

The producer owns truthful emission. Consumers own declaring and testing their assumptions. A domain owner owns business meaning. A named governance path owns how incompatible facts become a decision.

This answer is less simple than “the producer”. It has worked better for me because it matches the system we actually operate: data becomes a contract only when somebody sends it, somebody interprets it, and both sides can change without pretending the other one does not exist.
